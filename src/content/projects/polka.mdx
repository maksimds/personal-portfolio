<CaseIntro>

Polka is a personal project I designed and built from scratch: a native macOS app that lives in the MacBook notch. Move your pointer to the notch and a shelf opens, where you can keep files, folders, links, pictures and text, then drag them back out into any app. It was also my first time building a macOS app, and my first real project engineered with Claude Code. Polka went from an empty Xcode project to v1.0 in about a week. One week later, v2.0 added sending items to Macs nearby and folders on the shelf. It’s free and open source on GitHub, and more than 20 people use it now.

<ArrowLink href="https://github.com/maksimds/Polka">Polka on GitHub</ArrowLink>

{/* Problem */}

<CaseSection title="Problem: My files lived on the Desktop, my links lived in a dozen tabs, and I could never find either when I needed them">

Every time I’m in the middle of something, my files end up scattered across the Desktop, next to a pile of screenshots I forgot to delete, and my links get lost somewhere among a dozen browser tabs. Then, right when I actually need them, for example when I’m applying to jobs and need my resume, cover letter and portfolio link all at once, I can’t find them right away.

I’d end up searching Finder, scrolling through my browser history, or just downloading the same file again. It’s a small thing, but it gets frustrating fast, especially when you’re doing it ten times a day. Meanwhile, the notch at the top of my screen was always there and never did anything.

</CaseSection>

{/* Solution */}

<CaseSection title="Solution: A shelf in the notch, designed in Figma first and engineered with Claude Code">

Polka turns the notch into a little shelf. Move your pointer to the notch and the shelf opens. Drag in anything you want to keep handy, and drag it back out into any app whenever you need it. The goals for v1 were simple:

</CaseSection>

<CaseFeatures items={[

As a designer, I started where I always do: in Figma. I designed a mock-up of the shelf and mapped out each of its states: closed, open, and ready to catch something you’re dragging toward it.

Then I turned it into a prototype to see how opening, dropping and dragging would actually feel, and adjusted the sizes, timing and details until the shelf felt like a natural part of macOS rather than an extra window on top of it. That way I knew exactly what I wanted to build before writing a single line of code.

</CaseDetail>

<CaseDetail title="Hover to open, drag to catch">

The shelf opens after a short pause on the notch, so passing the pointer by on the way to the menu bar doesn’t trigger it. When you start dragging a file or link anywhere on the screen, the shelf opens on its own as a drop zone, and a pair of eyes watches your pointer and blinks until you let go.

<CaseDetail title="Your files never move">

Polka remembers where your files are instead of copying them, so your originals stay where they are and Polka even follows them if you rename or move them later. If a file gets deleted, its item shows as unavailable instead of quietly disappearing. For things you want to keep safe, **Keep a Copy** saves Polka’s own copy. New screenshots can go straight to the shelf, too.

Version 1.0 also had **Temporary** items that removed themselves after 24 hours. I took them out in 2.0: now everything simply stays on the shelf until you remove it.

</CaseDetail>

<CaseDetail title="Engineering it with Claude Code">

For the engineering side, I decided to dabble with Claude Code and build a native macOS app for the first time. I wrote a PRD, split the work into milestones, and built Polka step by step with Swift, SwiftUI and AppKit, testing every piece on my own Mac along the way. Every change went through its own branch and pull request, so the main version always worked.

The trickiest part was making the notch catch a drag the moment it starts anywhere on the screen, without ever stealing focus from the app you’re working in. My first version of the drop zone didn’t accept drops at all, because the system tried to give the shelf focus and the shelf refused. Switching to a lower-level way of receiving drops fixed it, and kept the shelf from ever taking focus.

</CaseDetail>

<CaseDetail title="Private by design">

Everything stays on your Mac, with no accounts and no cloud. Link previews never reach into your local network, and nothing you put on the shelf ends up in the app’s logs.

</CaseDetail>

<CaseSection title="Version 2.0: Sending to Macs nearby, and folders to keep the shelf tidy">

After v1.0, I used Polka every single day, and the more I used it, the more I noticed what was still missing. So I went back to the same process: design it first, then engineer it. One week later, Polka 2.0 was out. The biggest change is Nearby Sharing, then folders, plus a handful of smaller things that make the shelf feel even more like Finder.

</CaseSection>

<CaseDetail title="Nearby Sharing: designing it">

You can now send files, links and text from your shelf straight to another Mac’s shelf, without AirDrop, and the two Macs don’t even need to be on the same network.

{/* Image to add: the Send To box beside the shelf while an item is dragged
<CaseMedia variant="card" caption="Start dragging and the Macs nearby appear beside the shelf" images={[
  { src: "polka-send-box.png", alt: "[YOUR DESCRIPTION OF THE IMAGE]", retina: true },
]} />
*/}

As a designer, I wanted sending something to feel as natural as dropping it on the shelf. So when you start dragging an item, a small box with the Macs nearby appears right beside the shelf, and you just drop it on the one you want. You can also right-click any item and choose **Send To**.

On the other side, the shelf opens on its own and shows what’s arriving, with clear **Accept** and **Decline** buttons. **Decline and Block** turns a Mac away when someone you don’t know keeps sending things.

For Macs you trust, I designed a simple pairing flow: both screens show a 6-digit code, and if they match, everything between those two Macs arrives without asking from then on.

<CaseDetail title="Nearby Sharing: engineering it">

Engineering it with Claude Code was the most challenging part of the project so far. Since this feature lets another device send things to your Mac, it had to be safe, not just work. I built it in six small steps: finding Macs nearby, sending links and text, sending files with previews, progress and a cancel button, remembering trusted Macs, and the Send To box.

Then I finished by testing it against a fake “hostile” Mac that tried to break it, followed by a security review and a couple of fixes. Offers are capped at 50 items and 1 GB at a time, and you can set Polka to receive only from Macs you’ve remembered, so no one else can even see you. It was a good reminder that the features that look simplest on the surface often need the most care underneath.

<CaseSection title="Impact: Designed, built, shipped, and my Desktop is finally clean (mostly)" afterRule>

Polka was built for me first, so I honestly didn’t expect more than 20 people to start using it within a couple of weeks. It went from an idea and a Figma prototype to v1.0 in about a week, and to v2.0, with Nearby Sharing and folders, a week after that. It’s now the first place I drop a file instead of the Desktop. Along the way I delivered a PRD, a Figma mock-up and prototype, a native macOS app, a user guide in the README, and two free, open-source releases under the MIT license. I’m planning to keep improving it, so feature ideas are always welcome.

**Try it:** download Polka from the <a class="link" href="https://github.com/maksimds/Polka/releases" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Releases page on GitHub</a>.
